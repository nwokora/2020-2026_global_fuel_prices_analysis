Executive Summary
This analysis examined 84 countries across seven continental regions to assess global fuel price patterns, tax burden, and subsidy distribution. The results show clear regional inequalities in fuel affordability, with Europe and Oceania generally facing the highest fuel prices and tax burdens, while the Middle East records the lowest fuel prices and the strongest subsidy support.

Fuel prices vary sharply by region and country. Europe has the highest average fuel price at 3.33, followed closely by Oceania at 3.29, while the Middle East has the lowest average at 1.10. At the country level, Europe dominates the top 10 highest fuel-price countries, with eight countries in that group, while the Middle East and Africa dominate the lowest fuel-price countries. This confirms that fuel cost pressure is not evenly distributed across the world.

The yearly trend also shows worsening affordability over time. Average fuel prices increased from 2020 to 2026, with only a brief decline in 2023 before rising again. This suggests that fuel affordability has generally become more strained in recent years.

Tax burden is also uneven across regions. Oceania has the highest average tax percentage at about 39%, followed by Europe at about 35%, while South America and the Middle East record much lower averages at 18% and 20%, respectively. Rwanda stands out as the highest-taxing country at 39.6%, and Europe contains seven of the top 10 highest taxpayers, showing that higher fuel costs are often accompanied by stronger tax pressure.

Subsidy patterns point in the opposite direction. The Middle East has the highest average subsidy level at 3.00, followed by South America at 2.43 and Africa at 2.00, while Oceania and Europe have the lowest averages. The regional distribution reinforces this pattern: Oceania has 100% low subsidy, while the Middle East has the largest share of very high subsidies. Among low-income countries with very high subsidies, Venezuela, Libya, Egypt, and Nigeria appear most favoured in terms of fuel affordability, while Uganda, Mozambique, Zambia, Rwanda, and Zimbabwe appear least favoured and more vulnerable to affordability pressure.




Insights
The results suggest that fuel affordability is strongly shaped by geography, taxation, subsidy policy, and income structure.

Europe and Oceania face a double burden of high fuel prices and high taxes, but their higher income composition may cushion the impact of these costs.

The Middle East appears to benefit from lower fuel prices and stronger subsidy support, which improves affordability across the region.

Africa faces the greatest affordability pressure because it combines lower income levels, lower subsidy support in many cases, and weaker fiscal cushioning.

High- and middle-income countries seem to reduce the pressure caused by high taxes and low subsidies, especially in Europe and Oceania.

Overall, the analysis shows that fuel affordability is not only about fuel price alone, but also about the interaction between income level, tax burden, and subsidy support.
